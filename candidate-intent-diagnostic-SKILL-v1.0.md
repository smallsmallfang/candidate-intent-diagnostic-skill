# Candidate Intent Diagnostic

## 1. Purpose

Use this skill to analyze recruitment conversations and diagnose **what the candidate actually means by their words and behavior**.

The goal is not to predict whether a candidate will accept an offer with certainty.

The goal is to distinguish:

- genuine rejection
- conditional interest
- risk-blocked interest
- role-design concerns
- compensation/condition blockers
- negotiation
- relationship-preserving rejection

Then recommend the **next appropriate recruiter action**.

> Core principle: A candidate saying “no” does not necessarily mean “no interest.” Diagnose the type of “no.”

---

## 2. Input

Accept any combination of:

- recruiter ↔ candidate chat history
- interview notes
- offer discussion
- candidate questions
- rejection messages
- follow-up messages
- hiring-manager feedback
- candidate stated priorities and constraints

If evidence is insufficient, explicitly mark uncertainty rather than inventing intent.

---

## 3. Diagnostic Principles

### 3.1 Never infer intent from one sentence

Evaluate the combination of:

1. Explicit statements
2. Repeated questions
3. Behavioral signals
4. Decision consistency
5. Concessions or conditions proposed
6. Continued engagement
7. Willingness to invest communication effort
8. Relationship-preserving behavior

### 3.2 Separate interest from acceptance

A candidate can simultaneously have:

- high interest in the company
- high interest in the role
- low willingness to accept current conditions

Do not collapse these into a single “interested / not interested” label.

### 3.3 Separate candidate concerns from role defects

A candidate repeatedly asking about:

- authority
- resources
- reporting line
- success criteria
- decision rights
- workload
- organizational support

may be identifying a genuine role-design risk rather than being “difficult.”

### 3.4 Do not over-interpret politeness

Polite language alone is not evidence of interest.

Likewise, short or blunt language alone is not evidence of rejection.

Use the overall interaction pattern.

# 4. Intent Taxonomy

## A. Genuine Reject

### Typical signals
- Explicitly says they do not want the opportunity.
- Provides little or no conditional pathway.
- Stops investing communication effort.
- Does not seek additional role information.

### Recruiter action
- Respect the decision.
- Do not continue pressure.
- Confirm closure.
- Preserve the relationship where appropriate.
- Add to talent pool if relevant.

---

## B. Conditional Interest

### Typical signals
Candidate communicates a condition such as:

- “If X can be solved, I would consider it.”
- “If the role is structured differently, I am interested.”
- “If the compensation reaches X, we can continue.”
- “I need clarity on X before making a decision.”

### Diagnosis

The candidate is not rejecting the opportunity itself.

They are rejecting the **current version of the opportunity**.

### Recruiter action

Identify whether the condition is:

- negotiable
- structurally changeable
- impossible to change
- misunderstood

Do not repeatedly resell the opportunity before addressing the stated condition.

---

## C. Risk-Blocked Interest

### Typical signals

Candidate shows strong interest but repeatedly tests downside risk:

- Who has decision authority?
- Who owns the result?
- What resources are available?
- What happens when other departments disagree?
- What is the actual reporting relationship?
- What does success look like in 3–6 months?
- What happens if the organization does not provide required support?

### Diagnosis

The candidate may be willing to do the work but unwilling to accept an unclear or asymmetric risk structure.

### Recruiter action

Move from persuasion to risk clarification.

Provide concrete information about:

- authority
- resources
- reporting
- decision rights
- KPIs
- escalation paths
- organizational support

If these cannot be answered, escalate the issue to the hiring manager or organization owner.

---

## D. Role-Design Blocked

### Typical signals

The candidate's objections concern the architecture of the position itself:

- responsibilities exceed authority
- accountability exceeds resources
- role boundaries are unclear
- success criteria are undefined
- multiple stakeholders have conflicting expectations
- reporting structure is ambiguous

### Diagnosis

The obstacle may exist independently of the candidate.

### Recruiter action

Do not attempt to solve a structural problem with persuasion.

Escalate for role clarification or redesign.

---

## E. Compensation / Condition Blocked

### Typical signals

The candidate repeatedly focuses on:

- salary
- bonus
- equity
- working location
- working hours
- benefits
- leave
- flexibility
- other concrete employment conditions

### Diagnosis

The candidate may have high role interest but insufficient willingness to accept the current package.

### Recruiter action

Clarify:

- non-negotiable constraints
- negotiable items
- candidate's actual decision threshold

Do not assume compensation is the issue merely because compensation was discussed once.

---

## F. Negotiation

### Typical signals

The candidate:

- demonstrates interest
- provides alternatives
- proposes concrete conditions
- continues engagement
- asks what can be changed
- compares trade-offs

### Diagnosis

The candidate may be testing the organization's flexibility rather than rejecting the opportunity.

### Recruiter action

Determine:

1. Candidate's priorities
2. Organization's flexibility
3. Minimum acceptable conditions
4. Items that are genuinely non-negotiable

Avoid interpreting every counteroffer as low commitment.

---

## G. Relationship-Preserving Rejection

### Typical signals

The candidate rejects the current opportunity but:

- gives detailed reasons
- remains willing to communicate
- expresses respect for the recruiter/team
- continues casual or professional interaction
- leaves the door open to future opportunities

### Diagnosis

The candidate may be rejecting the **current opportunity**, not the **relationship**.

### Recruiter action

Respect the current decision.

Do not force conversion.

Preserve the relationship and consider future-fit opportunities.

---

# 5. Signal Library

## High-value positive engagement signals

These do not prove acceptance, but can indicate meaningful engagement:

- detailed questions
- repeated follow-up
- requests for concrete information
- willingness to speak with hiring managers
- proposing alternative structures
- discussing decision criteria
- explaining why a condition matters

## High-value rejection signals

- explicit final rejection
- refusal to continue discussion
- no alternative conditions
- no further questions
- repeated closure statements
- declining additional conversations

## Ambiguous signals

Do not over-interpret:

- slow replies
- short replies
- polite language
- emojis
- “再看看”
- “考虑一下”
- “目前不太合适”
- temporary silence

These require contextual evidence.

---

# 6. Candidate Decision Model

When possible, infer the candidate's decision model across these dimensions:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Interest | Does the candidate actually want the work/company? |
| Fit | Does the role match what they want to do? |
| Conditions | Are compensation and working conditions acceptable? |
| Risk | What downside does the candidate perceive? |
| Commitment | How much effort is the candidate investing? |
| Relationship | Does the candidate want to preserve contact? |

Use:

- High
- Medium
- Low
- Unknown

Do not fabricate missing values.

---

# 7. Diagnostic Procedure

Follow this sequence.

### Step 1 — Extract explicit statements

List what the candidate actually said.

Separate facts from interpretation.

### Step 2 — Extract behavioral signals

Identify:

- questions
- follow-ups
- concessions
- conditions
- refusals
- continued engagement

### Step 3 — Identify blockers

Classify blockers as:

- compensation
- role
- authority
- resources
- reporting
- workload
- location
- timing
- company risk
- personal constraint
- unknown

### Step 4 — Determine whether the blocker is negotiable

Classify each blocker:

- negotiable
- potentially negotiable
- structurally difficult
- non-negotiable
- unknown

### Step 5 — Determine intent type

Select one primary diagnosis and, if needed, secondary diagnoses.

Example:

> Primary: Risk-Blocked Interest  
> Secondary: Role-Design Blocked  
> Confidence: Medium

### Step 6 — Recommend recruiter action

Choose between:

- close
- clarify
- negotiate
- escalate
- pause
- preserve relationship
- revisit later

### Step 7 — Generate the next best question

The question should reduce the largest remaining uncertainty.

# 9. Guardrails

Do not:

- claim certainty about a candidate's internal thoughts
- diagnose personality or mental health
- infer intent from demographic characteristics
- equate slow replies with low interest
- equate negotiation with rejection
- equate detailed questioning with hostility
- pressure candidates after a clear final rejection
- manipulate candidates into accepting conditions they explicitly reject

Use evidence-based language:

Prefer:

> “The conversation suggests…”

Instead of:

> “The candidate definitely thinks…”

---

# 10. Core Heuristic

Use this as the primary reasoning rule:

> **Do not ask only “Does the candidate want the job?”**
>
> Ask:
>
> **“What version of this opportunity would the candidate be willing to accept, what prevents that version from existing today, and how much effort is the candidate still willing to invest in finding out?”**

The purpose of the skill is to turn ambiguous recruitment conversations into a structured diagnosis that helps HR decide whether to **close, clarify, negotiate, escalate, preserve, or revisit**.
